Guide 1: How to Calculate the Landed Cost of Imported Goods

Topic: Import costing · Audience: Importers and small businesses · Reviewed: 23 November 2026 · Publisher: MosesDigital

Landed cost is the combined cost of acquiring goods and bringing them to the point where the importer can receive or use them. It is more than the supplier's invoice price. A product that appears inexpensive at the factory can become costly after freight, insurance, customs taxes, handling, storage, financing and local delivery are included.

Step 1 — Establish the shipment value

Start with the supplier invoice and identify the currency, quantity, unit price, discounts and agreed delivery term. Check whether the quotation includes packing, inland transport, export clearance or other services. Do not assume two supplier quotations cover the same costs simply because the unit prices look similar.

Step 2 — Estimate the customs value

For a planning example, CIF means cost of goods plus international freight and insurance to the relevant destination point. The customs value used by the authority may require valuation adjustments and must be determined under the applicable customs valuation rules. Convert foreign currency using the exchange rate accepted for the customs declaration, not an arbitrary rate chosen only to make the estimate look favourable.

Illustrative example: Goods value KSh 500,000 + freight KSh 40,000 + insurance KSh 5,000 = illustrative CIF KSh 545,000. This example demonstrates addition only; it is not a customs valuation ruling.

Step 3 — Add taxes and statutory charges

Identify the correct HS classification, country of origin, applicable tariff measure, preferential treatment (if any), excise treatment, VAT treatment and other statutory levies. Rates and tax bases can differ by product and may be changed by legislation or legal notice. Do not apply one example rate to every imported product.

Step 4 — Add shipment and local costs

Collect written quotations for clearing-agent services, port or terminal handling, storage, inspection where applicable, bank charges, local transport, insurance extensions and other shipment-specific expenses. Separate recoverable taxes from costs that form part of the business's economic cost, depending on the business's tax position.

Step 5 — Calculate the unit landed cost

Add the costs that belong in the chosen costing model, then divide by the number of saleable units. If the shipment contains different products, allocate shared freight and handling costs using a reasonable method such as weight, volume, value or units, depending on what drives the cost.

Basic planning formula: Total estimated shipment cost ÷ saleable quantity = estimated landed cost per unit.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using the supplier invoice alone as the landed cost.
  • Ignoring freight, insurance, port storage or local delivery.
  • Applying a duty rate without checking the product's HS code and origin.
  • Confusing an estimate with the final customs assessment.
  • Dividing by ordered quantity when some units may be damaged, missing or unsaleable.
  • Failing to update exchange rates and supplier quotations before placing an order.

Guide 2: Understanding Import Duty, Excise Duty and VAT

Topic: Customs taxes · Audience: Importers and trade learners · Reviewed: 23 November 2026 · Publisher: MosesDigital

Importers often use the words duty, excise and VAT as if they were the same charge. They are different taxes with different legal rules, product coverage and calculation bases. The correct treatment depends on the importing country, tariff classification, product description, customs value, origin and the law in force on the declaration date.

Import duty

Import duty is generally determined by applying the tariff rate for the correctly classified goods to the customs value or other legally prescribed base. A tariff line may have a rate that differs from the general rate because of product-specific measures, origin, a preference, an exemption or a temporary legal notice.

Excise duty

Excise duty applies only to goods or activities specified by the applicable excise law. It is not a universal charge on every imported item. The rate and tax base depend on the product and legislation. Before using a percentage, confirm that the goods are excisable and that the selected base is legally correct.

Value Added Tax (VAT)

VAT on importation is calculated according to the importing country's VAT law and the prescribed import VAT base. The base may include customs value, customs duty and excise duty, together with other elements specified by law. The exact treatment must be checked against current legislation and customs guidance.

MosesDigital estimator convention: The current Kenya planning estimator uses the bases requested for this tool: import duty = CIF × entered duty rate; excise duty = (CIF + import duty) × entered excise rate; VAT = (CIF + import duty + excise duty) × entered VAT rate. These are displayed as an illustrative calculation model and must not be treated as a universal statutory formula for every HS code or every EAC Partner State.

Worked illustration using the estimator convention

Assume illustrative CIF of KSh 100,000, import duty rate of 10%, excise rate of 20%, and VAT rate of 16%:

StageIllustrative calculationAmount
Import duty100,000 × 10%KSh 10,000
Excise duty(100,000 + 10,000) × 20%KSh 22,000
VAT(100,000 + 10,000 + 22,000) × 16%KSh 21,120
Total of these three illustrative taxes10,000 + 22,000 + 21,120KSh 53,120

This simplified example excludes IDF, RDL, fees, exemptions, minimum amounts and any special tax treatment. It is a mathematical illustration of the stated estimator convention, not an official tax assessment or advice that these rates apply to a particular product.

How to verify the actual tax treatment

  1. Confirm the detailed product description, material, function and intended use.
  2. Determine and verify the HS code using the official tariff and classification guidance.
  3. Check the tariff rate and any origin-based preference or special measure.
  4. Check whether excise duty applies and identify its statutory base.
  5. Check the import VAT base, exemptions and any other levies.
  6. Confirm the applicable date and retain the official source or written advice used.

Official starting points: Kenya Revenue Authority and the EAC Online Customs Tariff.

Guide 3: A Practical Sequence for Importing Goods into Kenya

Topic: Import process · Audience: New and existing importers · Reviewed: 23 November 2026 · Publisher: MosesDigital

A successful import begins before the supplier receives payment. Importers should first confirm that the product can legally enter the country, estimate the full cost, agree clear commercial terms and understand the documents and inspections that may apply.

Before ordering

  1. Research demand: Identify the intended customer, competing products, expected selling price and realistic sales volume.
  2. Describe the goods precisely: Record materials, model, function, specifications, brand, packaging and intended use. These details may affect classification and regulatory requirements.
  3. Check controls: Search official procedures for permits, standards, health, safety, plant or animal controls, restricted goods and any pre-export conformity requirements.
  4. Verify the supplier: Independently check business identity, address, references and payment details.
  5. Obtain a complete quotation: Confirm currency, quantity, delivery term, named place, lead time, packaging and who pays each cost.
  6. Calculate the landed cost: Include taxes, freight, insurance, clearing, handling, storage and local transport.

During shipment planning

Agree the Incoterms rule and named place in the sales contract. Arrange transport and insurance appropriate to the cargo. Confirm which party will prepare export documents and which party will provide import permits or certificates. Send the clearing agent accurate product details and draft documents early enough to identify errors before arrival.

At arrival and clearance

Provide the required documents through the applicable customs and trade systems, respond to requests for information, arrange any required inspection and pay assessed charges through official channels. Keep proof of payment and the customs release documents. Do not rely on an informal message or a third party's screenshot as proof that goods have been cleared.

After release

Check the delivered quantity and condition against the purchase order and packing list. Record shortages or damage promptly, reconcile the final freight and clearance invoices, calculate the actual landed cost and retain the import records for the required period.

Important: Procedures depend on the goods and current rules. Use InfoTrade Kenya to research applicable procedures, agencies, permits and laws. Confirm the final requirements with the relevant authority or licensed professional.

Guide 4: Common Import and Customs Documents Explained

Topic: Trade documentation · Audience: Importers and exporters · Reviewed: 23 November 2026 · Publisher: MosesDigital

Documents support the commercial transaction, transport, customs declaration and regulatory checks. The exact list varies by product, country, origin, transport mode and trade arrangement. The following descriptions explain common documents; they are not a universal checklist for every shipment.

DocumentWhat it normally showsWhy it matters
Commercial invoiceSeller, buyer, goods description, quantity, unit price, currency and sale termsSupports the commercial value and transaction details

Document-checking routine

  • Make sure the buyer, seller and consignee names are consistent where required.
  • Compare quantities, product descriptions, marks and package counts across documents.
  • Check invoice currency, unit values, freight, insurance and delivery terms.
  • Ensure certificates and permits relate to the actual product and shipment.
  • Check issue dates, validity periods, signatures and issuing bodies.
  • Ask the relevant authority or clearing agent about discrepancies before declaration.

For Kenya-specific procedures, consult InfoTrade Kenya and the relevant regulator. For regional trade references, consult the East African Community.

Guide 5: HS Codes and Customs Tariff Classification

Topic: Tariff classification · Audience: Importers, exporters and trade students · Reviewed: 23 November 2026 · Publisher: MosesDigital

The Harmonized System (HS) is an international product nomenclature used as the foundation for customs tariff classification. Countries and customs unions may add further digits for more detailed national or regional tariff lines. The HS code affects the duty rate and can also point to permits, restrictions, trade statistics and other regulatory requirements.

Why a product description alone may not be enough

Everyday names can be broad or ambiguous. Classification may depend on the product's material, composition, function, degree of processing, technical features, packaging and intended use. Similar-looking goods can fall under different tariff headings. A supplier's suggested code is useful information but should not be accepted without independent verification.

A careful classification workflow

  1. Write a complete technical description of the goods.
  2. Collect product specifications, catalogues, photographs and composition details.
  3. Search the official tariff for candidate headings and legal notes.
  4. Read the relevant Section Notes, Chapter Notes and General Rules for Interpretation.
  5. Check the complete tariff line and any explanatory or classification guidance available.
  6. Where uncertainty has material financial or compliance consequences, seek a formal classification decision or advance ruling where available.
Do not classify by keyword alone. A search result or supplier invoice description does not replace the legal interpretation of the tariff. Keep a record of the facts and reasoning supporting the classification.

Use the EAC Online Customs Tariff as a regional starting point and verify the applicable national measures and current legal notices.

Guide 6: Preparing an Export Shipment from Kenya

Topic: Export readiness · Audience: Kenyan producers, traders and small businesses · Reviewed: 23 November 2026 · Publisher: MosesDigital

Exporting requires more than finding an overseas buyer. The exporter must confirm that the product can be exported, that it meets destination-market requirements, and that the commercial, payment, transport and documentation arrangements are workable.

Market and buyer checks

Research demand, competing suppliers, product standards, labelling, packaging, taxes and import restrictions in the destination market. Verify the buyer's legal identity, business address, references and payment arrangements. Agree specifications, inspection rights, delivery dates, currency, dispute handling and the Incoterms rule in writing.

Product and regulatory checks

Some products require export permits, health or phytosanitary certificates, quality certification, traceability records or special packaging. Agricultural, plant, animal, food, mineral and manufactured goods can have different controls. Ask the responsible Kenyan agency and the destination country's importer or authority to confirm the current requirements.

Shipment preparation

  1. Confirm the product classification and destination import requirements.
  2. Agree the buyer's order and commercial terms in writing.
  3. Prepare the invoice, packing list and certificates required for the goods.
  4. Book suitable transport and insurance.
  5. Arrange export customs formalities and any required inspection.
  6. Send the buyer the agreed shipping and document information.
  7. Track delivery, confirm receipt and reconcile payment.

Manage payment and delivery risk

Consider the buyer's creditworthiness, currency risk, bank charges, payment timing and the risk of non-payment. The appropriate payment method depends on the relationship, value, country and bargaining position. Do not release original documents or goods contrary to the agreed contract and bank instructions.

Useful starting points include KenTrade, KRA, KEBS and KEPHIS, depending on the product.

Guide 7: Simple Financial Records for a Small Business

Topic: Small-business management · Audience: Entrepreneurs and micro-businesses · Reviewed: 23 November 2026 · Publisher: MosesDigital

Reliable records help a business understand whether it is making money, identify customers who owe payment, manage stock and prepare information for tax and other obligations. A small business can start with a consistent daily routine and a simple spreadsheet or ledger.

Keep separate records for different activities

  • Sales: Date, invoice or receipt number, customer, product, quantity, amount and payment status.
  • Purchases: Supplier, date, item, quantity, invoice reference and amount paid or payable.
  • Expenses: Date, category, purpose, supplier and supporting receipt.
  • Cash and bank: Money received, payments made, transfers and reconciled balances.
  • Inventory: Opening stock, purchases, sales, returns, damage and closing stock.
  • Receivables and payables: Amounts customers owe and amounts due to suppliers.

Review the numbers monthly

Compare sales with the cost of goods sold and operating expenses. Review overdue customer balances, upcoming supplier payments and stock that is not moving. A cash balance is not the same as profit: a business may have cash from a loan or unpaid supplier bills, while a profitable sale may not yet have been paid by the customer.

Simple profit illustration: Sales KSh 80,000 less cost of goods sold KSh 45,000 less operating expenses KSh 20,000 = illustrative operating profit KSh 15,000 before any other applicable adjustments or taxes.

Build a practical record-keeping habit

  1. Record each transaction when it happens.
  2. Keep invoices, receipts and bank or mobile-money confirmations.
  3. Use consistent categories for income and expenses.
  4. Reconcile cash, bank and mobile-money balances regularly.
  5. Back up the records and restrict access to sensitive customer information.
  6. Review results monthly and correct errors promptly.

Guide 8: How to Check an Overseas Supplier Before Paying

Topic: Supplier due diligence · Audience: Importers and online sellers · Reviewed: 23 November 2026 · Publisher: MosesDigital

Supplier fraud and poor-quality goods can turn an otherwise attractive import into a loss. A professional-looking website, social-media account or digital certificate is not enough to prove that a supplier is genuine. Verification should use several independent checks.

Identity and business checks

  • Obtain the supplier's full registered business name, registration details and physical address.
  • Check the business against the relevant official company registry where accessible.
  • Call a telephone number obtained independently, not only the number in an unexpected payment message.
  • Check whether the website domain, email address and business identity are consistent.
  • Request verifiable trade references and confirm them independently.

Product and order checks

  • Request a detailed quotation and technical specifications.
  • Ask for current product photographs, samples or an independent inspection where proportionate.
  • Agree quality standards, packaging, quantity tolerance, delivery date and remedies for defects in writing.
  • Compare the offer with other suppliers and investigate unusually low prices.

Payment safety

Check bank beneficiary details through a known, independently verified contact before sending funds. Treat last-minute bank-account changes, pressure to pay immediately, requests to use an unrelated personal account and refusal to provide normal commercial documents as warning signs. For a first transaction, consider payment structures that reduce exposure and obtain advice suited to the transaction size.

Pause if details change. If a supplier suddenly changes bank details, email address, shipping agent or payment instructions, independently verify the change using a previously trusted contact channel before taking action.

Keep the quotation, contract, correspondence, payment proof, inspection records and shipping documents together. A clear paper trail makes it easier to resolve disputes and reconcile the final landed cost.

10. Understanding Incoterms

Incoterms clarify responsibilities between buyer and seller for delivery, costs and risk. Always use the named place and the Incoterms version in the contract.

  • EXW: Goods made available at the seller's premises.
  • FCA: Delivery to the agreed carrier or location.
  • FOB: Seller delivers goods on board the vessel under the sea transport rule.
  • CFR: Seller pays cost and freight to the named destination port.
  • CIF: Seller pays cost, insurance and freight to the named destination port.
  • DAP: Delivery to the agreed destination, ready for unloading.
  • DDP: Seller assumes delivery responsibilities including import clearance and duties under the agreed term.

11. International Shipping Methods

  • Air freight: Often suitable for urgent, lighter or higher-value cargo.
  • Sea freight: Often suitable for bulky shipments or larger quantities.
  • Courier: Useful for small parcels and express shipments.
  • Road freight: Common for regional trade within Africa.

Compare freight cost, transit time, insurance, reliability, handling and destination charges.

12. International Trade Payment Methods

  • Advance payment: Buyer pays before shipment; it can increase buyer risk.
  • Open account: Buyer pays later under agreed credit terms.
  • Documentary collection: Banks handle documents but do not guarantee payment.
  • Letter of credit: Bank payment undertaking subject to compliant documents and credit terms.

For larger transactions, discuss the risks and terms with your bank.
