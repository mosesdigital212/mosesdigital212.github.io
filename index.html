5. Understanding Landed Costs

Landed cost is the total cost of getting imported goods to their intended destination.

  • Purchase price.
  • International freight.
  • Insurance.
  • Customs duties.
  • Applicable taxes and levies.
  • Clearing and handling charges.
  • Local transportation.
  • Bank and payment charges.

Basic formula: Total landed cost ÷ quantity = landed cost per unit.

10. Understanding Incoterms

Incoterms clarify responsibilities between buyer and seller for delivery, costs and risk. Always use the named place and the Incoterms version in the contract.

  • EXW: Goods made available at the seller's premises.
  • FCA: Delivery to the agreed carrier or location.
  • FOB: Seller delivers goods on board the vessel under the sea transport rule.
  • CFR: Seller pays cost and freight to the named destination port.
  • CIF: Seller pays cost, insurance and freight to the named destination port.
  • DAP: Delivery to the agreed destination, ready for unloading.
  • DDP: Seller assumes delivery responsibilities including import clearance and duties under the agreed term.

11. International Shipping Methods

  • Air freight: Often suitable for urgent, lighter or higher-value cargo.
  • Sea freight: Often suitable for bulky shipments or larger quantities.
  • Courier: Useful for small parcels and express shipments.
  • Road freight: Common for regional trade within Africa.

Compare freight cost, transit time, insurance, reliability, handling and destination charges.

12. International Trade Payment Methods

  • Advance payment: Buyer pays before shipment; it can increase buyer risk.
  • Open account: Buyer pays later under agreed credit terms.
  • Documentary collection: Banks handle documents but do not guarantee payment.
  • Letter of credit: Bank payment undertaking subject to compliant documents and credit terms.

For larger transactions, discuss the risks and terms with your bank.

13. International Trade Glossary

  • HS Code: Harmonised commodity classification code.
  • Customs Duty: Duty assessed under applicable customs rules.
  • Freight Forwarder: Service provider arranging cargo movement.
  • Bill of Lading: Sea transport document.
  • Airway Bill: Air cargo transport document.
  • Certificate of Origin: Document certifying goods' origin when required.
  • MOQ: Minimum Order Quantity.
  • Lead Time: Period from order to delivery.
  • Demurrage: Charges that may apply when cargo/container exceeds permitted facility time.

18. Customs Valuation & Import Taxes

Import taxes and levies are assessed using applicable customs valuation rules and tax bases. Depending on the goods, applicable charges may include:

  • Customs/CIF value (Cost, Insurance and Freight).
  • Import Duty.
  • Excise Duty, where applicable.
  • Value Added Tax (VAT).
  • Import Declaration Fee (IDF).
  • Railway Development Levy (RDL).
  • Other applicable charges.

Basic EAC tax calculation structure

  • Import Duty: CIF × Import Duty Rate.
  • Excise Duty: (CIF + Import Duty) × Excise Rate.
  • VAT: (CIF + Import Duty + Excise Duty) × VAT Rate.

Rates and applicable tax bases may vary depending on product classification, exemptions and prevailing regulations. Always verify current requirements before calculating final import costs.

Practical Import & Export Learning Centre

MosesDigital publishes practical learning material to help small businesses, first-time traders and logistics professionals understand the steps involved in cross-border trade. These guides explain the workflow and the questions to resolve before committing money to a shipment.

Important: Customs rates, taxes, permits, standards and procedures depend on the product, tariff classification, country of origin, customs value and the rules in force on the declaration date. The examples below are educational. Always verify the current position with the relevant customs and regulatory authority before importing or exporting.

Guide 1: Understanding Kenya and EAC Import Duty, Excise Duty and VAT

Topic: customs valuation and tax estimation · Intended for: importers, small businesses and logistics learners

Before ordering goods, an importer should estimate the customs value and the taxes that may become payable at importation. The estimate is only as reliable as the information entered: product description, HS code, origin, customs value, applicable rates and any approved exemption or preference.

Step 1 — Establish the customs value

For a basic planning estimate, CIF means the cost of the goods plus insurance and freight to the relevant place of importation. Use the customs value accepted under the applicable valuation rules, not automatically the supplier's invoice total. Customs may require adjustments or supporting evidence.

Step 2 — Confirm the correct tariff classification and rates

Identify the product's Harmonized System (HS) classification using its material, function, composition, specifications and intended use. Check the full tariff line in the current EAC Common External Tariff and confirm the applicable rate, origin treatment, restrictions and any product-specific measures. A broad product name alone is not enough to establish a tariff code.

Step 3 — Calculate the core ad valorem taxes

For the simplified calculation sequence used in this estimator, let CIF be the customs value and let the rates be entered as decimals (for example, 25% = 0.25):

TaxIllustrative calculationWhat it means
Import dutyCIF × Import Duty RateDuty is calculated on CIF for this simplified estimator.
Excise duty(CIF + Import Duty) × Excise RateWhere excise applies, use the stated excise base and the applicable product rate.
VAT(CIF + Import Duty + Excise Duty) × VAT RateThis is the simplified VAT base used by the estimator.
Worked example — illustration only: Assume CIF = KSh 100,000, import duty rate = 10%, excise rate = 25%, and VAT rate = 16%. Import duty = KSh 10,000. Excise duty = (100,000 + 10,000) × 25% = KSh 27,500. VAT = (100,000 + 10,000 + 27,500) × 16% = KSh 22,000. The three illustrative taxes total KSh 59,500. This example excludes any other applicable levies, fees, penalties, port charges, agency costs or adjustments.

Step 4 — Check other possible charges

Depending on the goods, date and applicable law, an importer may also need to account for other statutory levies, import declaration or railway development charges, regulatory fees, inspection costs, port and terminal charges, storage, shipping-line fees, customs-agent fees and inland transport. Do not assume that the three core tax lines above represent every amount payable.

Step 5 — Keep evidence and reconcile the final entry

  • Keep the commercial invoice, packing list, transport document, insurance evidence and proof of payment.
  • Keep product specifications, catalogue pages, photographs and technical data used to support classification.
  • Check the customs entry and assessment against the declared HS code, customs value, origin and rates.
  • Compare the final landed cost with the original budget and record any variance for future quotations.

Useful official checks: EAC Online Customs Tariff, Kenya Revenue Authority and the InfoTrade Kenya portal.

Guide 2: How to Identify and Verify an HS Code

Topic: tariff classification · Intended for: importers, exporters, purchasing teams and customs learners

An HS code is a standardized product classification used by customs administrations. It helps determine the duty rate, trade statistics, licensing conditions, prohibitions, standards and other controls that may apply. Choosing a code based only on a supplier's suggestion or an internet search can lead to incorrect declarations and unexpected costs.

Information to collect before searching

  • Common and commercial name of the product.
  • Main material or ingredients and their percentages, where relevant.
  • Function, principal use and how the product operates.
  • Whether it is complete, unfinished, a part, an accessory, a set or a mixture.
  • Technical specifications, model number, product catalogue, photographs and safety or composition sheets where relevant.

A practical classification workflow

  1. Write a precise product description. Avoid vague terms such as “machine”, “chemical”, “accessory” or “spare part”.
  2. Search the current tariff using the most specific product terms and identify possible headings.
  3. Read the heading text, section notes, chapter notes and relevant subheading notes. Do not select a code based on the heading title alone.
  4. Apply the General Rules for Interpretation and any relevant legal notes to decide which heading and subheading describes the goods.
  5. Check the complete national or regional tariff line and confirm the current rate, measures, exemptions and documentation requirements.
  6. Save the reasoning and supporting product evidence. If classification remains uncertain, seek a formal ruling or written guidance from the competent customs authority or a qualified customs professional.

Common classification mistakes

  • Using a code from a different country without checking the applicable tariff extension.
  • Assuming goods made from similar materials always share the same code.
  • Classifying a part as a complete machine, or the reverse, without checking the legal notes.
  • Ignoring product composition, capacity, function or technical characteristics.
  • Reusing an old code after a tariff amendment without checking its validity.
Record-keeping tip: Maintain a product classification file for each SKU with the description, photographs, specifications, proposed HS code, source consulted, date checked, rate verified and the name of the person or authority that confirmed it.

Start with the EAC Online Customs Tariff and review the official EAC rules and regulations. For a disputed or high-value classification, do not rely on an informal web search as a binding decision.

Guide 3: Kenya Import Process and Customs Clearance Checklist

Topic: shipment preparation and clearance · Intended for: businesses importing into Kenya

Successful clearance begins before the supplier dispatches the goods. Importers should establish what the product is, whether it is restricted or regulated, which documents are required, and how the shipment will be declared and delivered.

Before placing the order

  1. Describe the product accurately and confirm its likely HS classification.
  2. Check whether the product is prohibited, restricted or subject to a permit, licence, registration, inspection or conformity requirement.
  3. Confirm the required standards and approvals with the relevant regulator. Depending on the product, this may involve KRA, KEBS, KEPHIS, health, communications, energy or other competent authorities.
  4. Agree the Incoterm, named place, currency, payment terms, insurance responsibility and who will arrange freight and clearance.
  5. Obtain a written quotation for freight, insurance, customs-agent services, port handling and inland delivery.
  6. Estimate landed cost using a verified tariff classification and current official rates.

Documents commonly required

DocumentPurpose / check
Commercial invoiceSeller, buyer, description, quantity, unit price, currency, terms and total value.
Packing listPackage count, marks, weights, dimensions and contents.
Bill of lading or air waybillEvidence of carriage and shipment details.
Certificate of originOrigin evidence where required or where preferential tariff treatment is claimed.
Permits, licences or product approvalsRegulatory authorization applicable to the product.
Insurance and freight evidenceSupports valuation and shipment cover where applicable.
Product specifications / conformity documentsSupports classification, standards and regulatory checks.

This is a planning list, not a universal document requirement. The exact documents vary by commodity, origin, transport mode, trade agreement and current Kenyan requirements.

During shipment and clearance

  1. Send complete and consistent documents to the appointed customs agent before arrival where possible.
  2. Check that invoice, packing list and transport document agree on descriptions, quantities, weights, consignee and shipment references.
  3. Ensure the customs declaration uses the correct classification, customs value, origin and supporting documents.
  4. Respond promptly to requests for additional information, examination or regulatory inspection.
  5. Review the customs assessment and pay only through authorized official channels.
  6. Obtain release confirmation and coordinate collection, port or terminal release, and delivery.

After delivery

  • Keep the customs entry, receipts, permits and supporting documents in an organized shipment file.
  • Check received quantities and condition against the purchase order and packing list.
  • Record demurrage, storage, inspection, clearance and transport costs in the landed-cost record.
  • Investigate unexpected assessments or discrepancies promptly using the official review or dispute channels.

Useful official starting points: KRA, InfoTrade Kenya, KenTrade and KEBS.

Guide 4: Export Documentation and Procedure Planning

Topic: export readiness and documents · Intended for: Kenyan businesses selling to regional and international markets

Export requirements depend on the product, destination market, origin rules, buyer requirements, transport mode and any controls in force. A business should verify destination-country requirements before accepting an order or promising a delivery date.

Plan the export transaction

  1. Research the destination market, customer demand, product standards, labelling, packaging and import restrictions.
  2. Confirm whether the product requires an export permit, health or phytosanitary certificate, inspection, registration or other approval.
  3. Agree product specifications, quantity, price, currency, payment method, delivery schedule and Incoterm with the buyer.
  4. Confirm the correct HS classification and check whether the buyer needs a certificate of origin or evidence for preferential tariff treatment.
  5. Choose a transport mode and obtain quotations for freight, insurance, handling and destination charges.
  6. Prepare a cash-flow plan that includes production, packaging, certification, inland transport and the time between dispatch and payment.

Common export documents

DocumentTypical role
Pro forma invoice / sales contractRecords the proposed sale, specifications, price and agreed terms.
Commercial invoiceRecords the final sale and value for customs and payment purposes.
Packing listDescribes packages, contents, weights and dimensions.
Export customs declarationProvides shipment information to the exporting customs administration.
Bill of lading / air waybill / road consignment noteTransport document for the selected mode.
Certificate of originConfirms origin where required by the buyer or destination customs authority.
Health, phytosanitary, inspection or conformity certificateConfirms product-specific regulatory requirements where applicable.
Insurance certificateEvidence of cargo insurance when arranged or required.

Origin and preferential trade

A product does not automatically qualify for a preferential tariff simply because it is shipped from Kenya or another EAC Partner State. The product must satisfy the applicable rules of origin for the relevant trade arrangement, and the exporter must obtain the required proof of origin through the proper process. Check the product-specific origin rule, processing requirements, cumulation rules and documentary requirements before quoting a preferential rate.

Before dispatch checklist

  • Buyer and seller details match across the commercial documents.
  • Product descriptions, quantities, package counts and weights are consistent.
  • Required permits and certificates are valid and available.
  • Destination labelling, packaging and standards have been checked.
  • Incoterm, insurance, freight payer and risk-transfer point are understood.
  • Payment and document-release arrangements are agreed and independently verified.
  • Copies of all documents are saved and sent to the buyer, carrier and customs agent as required.

Check the EAC trade portal, EAC Trade Helpdesk and InfoTrade Kenya for official procedures and country-specific information.

How to use these guides

Use each guide as a preparation checklist, then confirm the live legal and operational requirements for the specific product and transaction. Keep a dated record of the official sources consulted. If a rule, tariff, permit or procedure changes, update your shipment plan before proceeding.
